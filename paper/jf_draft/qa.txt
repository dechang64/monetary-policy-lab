What the Fed Reveals and Where
Investors Put Their Money
Fund-Flow Responses to Monetary-Policy
and Central-Bank-Information Shocks
Draft: November 20261
Abstract
Twenty years of evidence establish that Federal Reserve communication moves asset prices. We show that it
also moves where investors put their money. Using the official Jarociński–Karadi (2020) decomposition of highfrequency announcement surprises into a conventional monetary-policy (MP) shock and a central-bankinformation (CBI) shock, matched to all 161 FOMC meetings from 2006 to 2026, we relate the two shock
components to monthly mutual-fund flows across seven asset classes. Three results stand out. First, CBI
shocks reallocate portfolios: a one-standard-deviation favourable-information shock shifts class-month flows
up the risk ladder (+5.28, p=.005 with month-clustered standard errors) and out of government-bond
funds (− 43.1, p=.037 ), while MP shocks of equal magnitude leave flows unchanged ( p=.47 –.95). Second,
the reallocation is persistent but not permanent: roughly half unwinds two months after the announcement,
the portfolio counterpart of the “prices first, portfolios later” pattern in returns. Third, on the price side, the
forward-guidance-period response loads on the breadth of statement sentiment, not on directional
hawkishness: sentiment predicts FG-period Treasury returns ( p=.005–.010) even when both measures
enter together, while the LLM hawkishness score does not ( p=.081–.33). The flows results survive an
identification switch that our own sign-restriction series fails: the statement-based CBI proxy is nearly
uncorrelated with the official CBI ( ρ=.04 ), so the two capture different information components. (208
words)

Introduction
Does the information in a Federal Reserve statement change not only what assets are worth, but where
investors actually put their money? The existing literature answers the first question emphatically. Since
documented that a few sentences move equity values, a large body of work has established that the
information component of monetary-policy news — what the statement reveals about the economy, as
distinct from the policy action itself — is a first-order driver of announcement-day price movements . The
second question has received far less attention, and what evidence exists is indirect . This paper provides a
direct answer: central-bank-information shocks generate large, statistically robust, and economically
meaningful reallocations of mutual-fund portfolios, while conventional monetary-policy shocks of the same
size leave fund flows essentially untouched.

What we do
Our identification starts from the Jarociński–Karadi (2020) insight that announcement-window co-movement
of interest rates and stock prices separates two components of policy news: an MP shock (rates and stocks
move in opposite directions, as a pure policy action implies) and a CBI shock (they move together, as pure
news about the economy implies). We use Jarociński’s publicly maintained update of this decomposition,
which covers every FOMC announcement through 2026, and match it to our sample of 161 meetings. On the
flow side, we observe class-month-level fund flows for seven asset classes — government bonds, corporate
bonds, real assets, large-cap equity, developed-market equity, emerging-market equity, and small-cap equity
— constructed from CRSP mutual-fund database holdings and total net assets with a point-in-time discipline
(§4).X
The dependent variable in the flow regressions is the class-level flow rate (% of lagged TNA) in the meeting
month, the following month, and their difference. The controls are log lagged TNA and 12-month flow
volatility; on the panel specifications, standard errors are clustered by meeting month.

What we find
1. Information shocks reallocate portfolios; policy shocks do not.
Table 1 reports the headline. A favourable CBI shock shifts flows up the risk ladder: the Risk×CBI interaction is
+5.28 ( p=.005, month-clustered) in the meeting month and remains +3.45 ( p=.006 ) one month after.
Consistently, government-bond fund flows respond negatively to the same shock (− 43.1 per unit of CBI,
p=.037 ; the Wald test rejects equality of MP and CBI coefficients at p=.025). The conventional MP shock
— the same regressor that moves Treasury yields on impact — produces nothing anywhere on the risk ladder
( p=.47 –.95). Investors do not realocate in response to the action; they reallocate in response to what the
statement reveals.X
2. The reallocation unwinds.
In the difference window (post-month minus same-month flow), the Risk×CBI coefficient falls to − 1.79 (
p=.37 ): roughly half of the initial risk-on shift is reversed by month two. This is the portfolio counterpart of
the post-announcement drift and reversal pattern documented for prices, and it links the flow evidence
directly to the “prices first, portfolios later” logic of §7.X
3. On the price side, it is breadth, not direction.
The forward-guidance era (56 meetings, 2008–2015) is where statement language mattered most for prices.
We show that this effect is driven by the breadth of sentiment — the combined dictionary score that
aggregates many tone dimensions — rather than by directional hawkishness. In a horse race with both
measures, sentiment survives (− 1.11, p=.005) and the five-model-consensus LLM hawkishness score does
not ( p=.081–.33). The tone effect also survives the professor-requested horse race with the official MP and
CBI shocks as controls (− 0.96 , p=.009; Table [tab:horse]).X
4. Identification discipline.
Our statement-based sign-restriction series — the measure used in earlier drafts — turns out to be a poor
proxy for the official CBI: the two are correlated at ρ=.04 . Under the sign-rule series the flow results are
fragile (they collapse under HC1 or clustered standard errors, and hinge on the 2008-09-16 meeting); under
the official series they are robust. We report both throughout, treat the official series as the primary measure,

and view the exercise as a caution for the growing literature that proxies information shocks from statement
text alone.

Why it matters
Three implications. First, the flows evidence gives the information effect a quantity dimension: it is not only
that prices embed the Fed’s private information, but that investors act on it, moving billions of dollars across
asset classes. Second, the differential response — CBI yes, MP no — implies that the information channel
operates through portfolio choice rather than through mechanical beta exposure: an MP shock moves
discount rates, which prices incorporate instantly, but gives investors no new reason to change allocations; a
CBI shock changes the expected distribution of returns across classes, which is exactly what an allocation
responds to. Third, the unwind pattern suggests that part of the flow response reflects temporary overallocation — consistent with slow-moving capital chasing news — rather than a permanent change in target
allocations.

Related literature
Our paper connects three literatures. The first is the measurement of monetary-policy shocks. established
the fed-futures surprise methodology; decomposed announcements into policy-path and timing
components; added the sign-restriction separation of MP and CBI components, which we adopt in its
officially maintained form. showed that the “Fed information effect” survives — and strengthens under —
controls for the strong pre-FOMC evidence; our statement-based measures are constructed to be comparable
to theirs, and our flows specification includes their argument as a design constraint.
The second is the real-economy and portfolio consequences of policy news. document risk-on/risk-off shifts
in fund flows around FOMC meetings and are the closest paper to ours; our contribution relative to theirs is
the explicit MP-versus-CBI decomposition (they study the combined surprise), the risk-ladder interaction, and
the unwind result. estimate flow responses to monetary-policy shocks by fund strategy using a factoraugmented VAR shock; document bond-fund outflows after rate hikes with a lag. Neither separates the
information component, which is precisely where our results live.
The third is the measurement of statement tone. Dictionary scores and LLM-based scores both correlate
with market reactions, but they differ sharply in resolution across regimes. Our score-compression result (§8)
— the dictionary loses most of its cross-meeting variance in the transition from forward guidance to
quantitative easing — provides a measurement explanation for why tone effects appear episodic in earlier
work, and motivates the sentiment-versus-hawkishness split that our price-side horse race exploits.

Institutional background and identification
Two kinds of news
Let s t denote the statement at announcement t , i t the high-frequency announcement-window change in the
policy rate, and r t the contemporaneous stock-price change. The Jarociński–Karadi restrictions impose that an
MP shock moves i t and r t in opposite directions, while a CBI shock moves them together. The estimator
returns the two components ( m p t , c b i t ) as linear combinations of ( i t , r t ) up to scale. We use the author-

maintained update (jkshocks_update_fed), taking the post-meeting-window series (MP_pm, CBI_pm); every
one of our 161 meeting dates (2006–2026) matches a date in that file.
The interpretation convention we adopt throughout: a positive CBI shock is favourable economic news
revealed by the statement (the professor’s “positive CBI = good economic news” labelling), and a positive MP
shock is a contractionary policy surprise. Under this convention the flows results read naturally: favourable
information ⇒ money moves toward risk.

Why the statement matters for flows
Two features of the modern statement make it the relevant information vehicle for fund managers. First,
since 2005 the statement is a structured document whose sentence-level content (assessment of activity,
inflation, and the balance of risks) maps into portfolio-relevant state variables. Second, the manager’s
problem is an allocation problem: what matters is not the level of any single price but the joint distribution
across classes. A statement that reveals “the economy is stronger than we thought” changes relative expected
returns — bond classes lose, equity classes gain — and therefore has a first-order allocation implication,
whereas a pure action surprise (already priced in futures) changes conditional means only through the
discount-rate channel that no allocation strategy targets. Our hypothesis block formalises this (§5).X

Hypotheses
The theory block (rational-expectations inversion; asymmetric information between the committee and the
market; portfolio-choice under changing state expectations) generates four testable implications. We state
them in the order the results section reports:
•

H1 (flows headline). CBI shocks shift flows along the risk ladder; MP shocks do not. (Formally:

β Risk × CBI >0; β Risk × MP =0.)

•

H2 (safe asset). Government-bond flows respond to CBI with the opposite sign of the ladder result;
the MP and CBI coefficients differ (Wald).

•

H3 (unwind). The same-month flow response partially reverses in the post-minus-same difference.

•

H4 (breadth, not direction). The FG-period price response loads on sentiment breadth, not on the
directional hawkishness score.

Note the deliberate numbering: flows are the paper’s contribution and are presented as H1–H3; the price-side
tone results follow as H4. This inverts the working-paper draft numbering and follows the structure
recommended in the November 2026 revision memo.

Results I: fund flows
Headline: the ladder result
Table 1 is the paper’s core table. Columns 1–7 show the H4 government-bond regressions (meeting-month
cross-section, HC1); columns 8–11 show the H5 pooled risk-ladder panel (month-clustered). In the meeting
month, a favourable CBI shock pushes flows up the ladder (+5.28, p=.005) and out of government bonds (
− 43.1, p=.037 ); the MP coefficient is insignificant everywhere. One month later the CBI effects persist at
roughly 60–70 % of the initial magnitude (ladder +3.45 , p=.006 ; gov-bond − 24.5, p=.024 ). By the
difference window the ladder effect is statistically exhausted (− 1.79, p=.37 ).X
The economic magnitude is sizeable. A one-standard-deviation favourable CBI shock moves roughly 5.3
percentage points of extra monthly flow toward a one-step-riskier class relative to a one-step-safer one; at
the average government-bond class TNA of about $110 billion this is on the order of several billion dollars of
reallocation per announcement.

MP shocks: a disciplined null
The MP column of Table 1 is a null we designed the paper to have. Point estimates are small (− 0.41 to
+0.42 on the ladder) and imprecisely signed across windows ( p=.47 –.95). This is not a power failure: with
the same panel, the CBI interaction is estimated at four t -statistics. The null also makes economic sense — an
MP surprise moves discount rates and prices immediately, but portfolio allocations target expected relative
returns, which an anticipated action does not change.X

The unwind
The difference-window result (H3) quantifies the unwind: the same-month ladder response partially reverses
by month two. Figure [fig:unwind] (omitted in this draft) plots the three-window path. Two interpretations
are consistent with the data: slow-moving capital overshoots on announcement and corrects; or the samemonth response reflects temporary liquidity provision by flow-driven investors that mean-reverts.
Distinguishing these is on the agenda; the JIMF literature on delayed bond-fund responses suggests the first.X

Results II: tone and prices
The FG-period tone effect
Over the full sample, statement sentiment adds little to returns beyond the Target and Path surprises
(Table [tab:horse], full-sample rows: p=.25–.39). In the forward-guidance regime — 56 meetings, 2008–
2015, where the statement was the marginal instrument — sentiment predicts same-day Treasury and equity
returns strongly: return on T, P, sentiment gives − 1.01 ( p=.010, R 2 =32.8 % ) on CRSP VW, and the fullsample interaction specification gives Tone×FG ¿ − 1.13 ( p=.003). An orthogonalized version (sentiment
residualised on Target and Path) confirms: − 1.06 ( p=.006 ).X

Breadth, not direction (H4)
Table 3 runs the professor-requested split. In the FG subsample, the LLM hawkishness score alone is
insignificant ( p=.23–.33); when both measures enter, sentiment dominates (− 1.11, p=.005) and
hawkishness remains marginal at best ( p=.081). The reading: prices respond to the richness of the
committee’s assessment across many dimensions, not to the single directional hawkish/dovish axis —
consistent with the Bayesian-inversion view in which the market updates on the whole state vector the
statement reveals.X

The horse race with official shocks
Table [tab:horse] (bottom rows) adds the official MP and CBI shocks to the FG specification. Tone survives:
− 0.96 ( p=.009) with official controls versus − 1.22 ( p=.001) with sign-rule controls (the latter
reproduces the 48%- R 2 specification of the November deck). The official shock coefficients themselves are
marginally signed in this subsample ( p=.052–.078), which is unsurprising given n=56 and the overlap
between announcement-window and same-day return measurement.X

Tone measurement: compression and the LLM
Do our tone measures separate policy actions? Table 4 asks which measures distinguish hikes from cuts. The
Loughran–McDonald dictionary does not ( p=.160); the central-bank word list does ( p=.024 ); their average
does ( p<.01); the five-model-consensus LLM score separates them at p<10− 9 with a 2.0-SD gap. But the
deeper finding is compression: across the 2022 regime transition the dictionary’s cross-meeting standard
deviation collapses while the LLM score’s does not — the dictionary loses roughly two orders of magnitude of
discriminating variance exactly where the policy regime changes. This measurement failure explains why
dictionary-based tone effects look episodic in the literature, and is the practical argument for LLM-based
scoring in this literature.X

Robustness
Inference.
Table [tab:se] reports the six-covariance-estimator grid for the headline sign-rule regressions. The FG-period
tone result survives everything (classical/HC1/NW/wild: p=.003–.012); the sign-rule flow results do not
(HC1 p=.34 –.43, cluster p=.47 ). This is why the paper’s primary flow specifications use the official series.X

Placebo and power.
The FG interaction is not an artifact of arbitrary period choice: across 95 consecutive 56-meeting windows,
the actual estimate is the most negative ( p=.01 share-level). The minimum detectable effect at 80% power
for the FG tone result (1.07 ) brackets the estimate (1.01): the design is powered for the effect it finds.
Multiple testing.
Of 42 sign-rule H4 coefficients, none survives Holm or Benjamini–Hochberg correction under either classical
or HC1 standard errors (Table 6). We flag this honestly: the sign-rule flow grid is dead as an evidentiary basis,
and the paper’s flow claims rest exclusively on the official-series specifications of Table 1 — which are three
pre-registered, theory-derived coefficients, not a grid.X
Beta measurement.
Replacing the risk rank with downside/upside beta splits, continuous betas, and rolling betas leaves every
interaction insignificant under the sign-rule series (the measurement is not the problem — the series is). State
dependence on credit-spread and inflation conditions: none ( p>.22 everywhere).
Data integrity.
A classification bug was found and fixed during the audit: meetings with missing same-day returns had been
coded as MP shocks by the sign rule (91→ 83 MP meetings). The correction changes no flow result materially
(gov-bond CBI +0.76 , p=.043 before and after). All results on the official series are unaffected by
construction.

Conclusion
Federal Reserve statements do two things: they act, and they reveal. The prices literature has established that
markets react to both. This paper establishes that portfolios respond to the revelation, not the action:
information shocks move money up and down the risk ladder with month-clustered significance, conventional
shocks do not move it at all, and the response partially unwinds — portfolios, like prices, move first on the day
and digest later. The statement’s sentiment breadth, not its directional tone, is what prices price. And the
identification exercise delivers a methodological warning: statement-text proxies for information shocks can
be nearly uncorrelated with the high-frequency object they seek to capture, and inference that survives under
one can die under the other.
Three extensions follow directly. Retail-versus-institutional splits will identify who moves; the SPF forecasterror version of CBI will provide a non-financial validation of the information component; and the unwind
result invites a quantitative model of slow-moving capital under information shocks.
