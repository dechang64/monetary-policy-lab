# 1. Introduction

How does Federal Reserve communication move asset prices, and when does it move the portfolios of households and institutions? The question is old; our answer is an identification. We decompose monetary-policy news into a conventional component (MP) and an information component (CBI) by letting financial markets invert the central bank's statement — the same-day market response, read through the Jarociński-Karadi sign structure, *is* the decomposition. The sign restriction is not an assumption layered on top of the data; it is the inversion rule that rational expectations implies, stated as a one-line classification on the shock file.

**The theoretical anchor.** Under rational expectations, the announcement-day price response is the market's Bayesian posterior update given the statement. It follows that any analyst-side measure of what the statement "said" — dictionary scores, LLM scores, human readings — measures the *text*, not the *market's information*. Pillar 1 disciplines everything that follows: text measures and inversion measures must be kept separate, and a text measure that correlates with the market's inversion outcome is suspect by construction. Pillar 2 supplies the decomposition tradition (Kuttner's window; Bauer-Swanson's oracle; Jarociński-Karadi's sign identification) and locates our contribution: statements, decomposed, with the one-line rule. Pillar 3 adds the portfolio side — a risk-ladder model in which information shocks reprice risk premia and flows reallocate along a 1–7 asset-class risk rank.

**What we find.**
1. *Prices* react to both components, and the dictionary-measured *tone* of the statement matters for prices only in the forward-guidance era — on equities: CRSP VW β = −1.119 (p = 0.004), S&P 500 β = −1.027 (p = 0.006), tone-only R² = 14%; in a horse race with both shocks, all three regressors are significant and the R² reaches 48%; on 10-year futures the FG interaction is positive (+0.050, p = 0.030) — hawks lift yields and sink valuations simultaneously. Outside FG, tone is dead on every asset.
2. *Portfolios* do not: neither shocks nor tone nor LLM-scored text add explanatory power to monthly fund flows (H4: p = 0.64 raw; p = 0.17 LLM). This null is the paper's ruling result — "prices first, portfolios later."
3. The risk-taking channel runs on *hawkish information*: positive CBI shocks — the market reading the announcement as the central bank acting on information it holds, typically bad news about the economy — drive de-risking along the risk ladder (Risk × CBI = −0.066, p = 0.036; sign-asymmetric: Risk × CBI(+) = −0.088, p = 0.020 vs Risk × CBI(−) = −0.055, p = 0.10; government-bond CBI flow beta = +0.76, p = 0.037). Portfolios respond to what the Fed *knows*, not to what it *does* — with a zero-rate-regime exception (MP × ZLB = −1.505, p = 0.005) that reveals the conventional channel working through rescue capacity.
4. *Measurement*: the LM dictionary fails by compression (not by mis-reading) exactly when the Fed's communication paradigm shifts — on the 11 divergence statements the dictionary output is nearly constant (std 1.10) while multi-model LLM scoring separates directions sharply (std ≈ 70); sign agreement across four vendor families is 100% on those statements. The LLM measure is positioned as a diagnostic of dictionary failure, not a better scorer.

**Contribution.** Three claims. (i) *Identification*: the inversion rule, stated as a reproducible one-line classification, gives statement-level MP/CBI decomposition without proprietary infrastructure. (ii) *Mechanism*: the flow null is not a limitation but evidence — with a theoretical reason (timing structure: prices are instantaneous, flows are monthly, and what survives to the monthly horizon is risk content, not words) and a reversal pattern in the window decomposition that pins the reallocation dynamics. (iii) *Measurement*: the resolution-loss signature, its five-layer audit, and the cross-vendor confirmation discipline the tone literature exactly where its objects are moving — the paradigm-shift episodes.
