# 1. Introduction

Since 1994, when the FOMC first began announcing its policy decision on the day of the meeting, and especially since 2005, when the statement became a structured instrument of guidance rather than a summary of action, the Federal Reserve's press release has been the primary vehicle through which monetary policy is communicated. By the zero-lower-bound era the statement had become, in a precise sense, the policy itself: with the instruments exhausted, what remained to move was the committee's language. Yet the economics of that language — what it moves, when, and through which channel — remains contested. This paper answers the question with an identification rather than a new text score, and its answer is the title: prices first, portfolios later.

**The identification.** We decompose monetary-policy news into a conventional component (MP) and an information component (CBI) by letting financial markets invert the central bank's statement. Under rational expectations, the announcement-day price response is the market's Bayesian posterior update given everything it knew; the sign pattern of that response reveals whether the market read the announcement as *action* (the classical policy surprise, moving rates and equity premia together) or as *revelation* (the central bank acting on private information, in the sense of Jarociński and Karadi 2020 and Coibion-Gorodnichenko-Ulig). Operationally, the rule is one line: a target shock whose sign agrees with the same-day CRSP market response is classified as information; one whose sign disagrees is classified as conventional. No proprietary infrastructure is required — the decomposition is auditable from two columns of a CSV file, which we regard as a feature: the inversion rule is exactly as transparent as the theory that justifies it.

**The design.** With the decomposition in hand, we trace both components — and the statement's own dictionary-measured tone — into (i) same-day prices across the Treasury curve and the equity market and (ii) monthly fund flows across a seven-class risk ladder from government bonds to small-cap equity. The panel spans 161 meetings from 2006 through 2026: the pre-crisis tightening, the crisis, the zero-bound and forward-guidance years, the 2020 pandemic response, the 2022–2023 cycle, and the current run-off. Three regimes — forward guidance (56 meetings), the 2020–2022 zero-rate window, and the post-2022 tightening cycle — let us ask not only whether communication moves assets but *when*, which is where the paper's answer lives.

**Finding 1: the text moves prices only when the words are the instrument.** Figure 1 previews the central result. Within the forward-guidance era, a one-standard-deviation hawkish shift in statement tone moves the same-day CRSP value-weighted return by −1.12 percentage points (β = −1.119, p = 0.004; S&P 500 −1.027, p = 0.006); outside that era the coefficient is +0.04 and dead (p = 0.80). The sign pattern across assets is the textbook one: hawks sink valuations and — through the regime interaction — lift ten-year yields (+0.050, p = 0.030), while leaving the two-year untouched: tone operates through the *medium-horizon path* expectations that forward guidance manages. Crucially, tone is not a proxy for the shocks: orthogonalizing it to Target+Path is a near no-op (first-stage R² = 0.4%), and in a horse race on FG-era equities all three regressors survive jointly (tone −1.21 p < 0.001; MP −1.25 p < 0.001; CBI +1.93 p = 0.004; R² = 48%). The statement's text is a separate information channel — the strongest form of our second testable implication.

**Finding 2: the words stop at the portfolio.** Neither the shocks nor any text measure moves monthly fund flows: tone p = 0.64 (raw), 0.63 (orthogonalized), 0.94 (FG regime); the audited LLM score p = 0.17; the shocks' direct effects p = 0.92 and 0.29. This null is deliberate and load-bearing — the theory (Section 2, Pillar 3) predicts it, because prices have already cleared the information by the time a monthly window opens. Where the earlier literature might read "no tone effect on flows" as a measurement failure, the framework reads it as the mechanism: prices first, portfolios later.

**Finding 3: what reaches portfolios is risk, not words.** Flows respond to the information component alone, along a risk ladder: Risk × CBI = −0.066 (p = 0.036) with Risk × MP a precise zero (+0.005, p = 0.88). Positive CBI shocks — the market reading the announcement as the Fed acting on information, typically bad news about the economy — drive **de-risking**: the safe anchor class receives (+0.332, p = 0.019; government bonds +0.76, p = 0.037) while classes higher on the ladder lose. The asymmetry is in sign, not magnitude: Risk × CBI(+) = −0.088 (p = 0.020) against Risk × CBI(−) = −0.055 (p = 0.10) — funds flee *risk* on bad news and do not chase the policy on good news. The one conventional-channel exception is the zero-rate window, where MP × ZLB = −1.505 (p = 0.005): at the bound a conventional surprise is an *exit-option* event, and it works through rescue capacity rather than news. This reverses our own proposal-stage H5 — announced there as risk-taking on expansionary surprises — and the reversal is the paper's empirical dividend: portfolios respond to what the Fed *knows*, not to what it *does*.

**Finding 4: the dictionary fails by compression, exactly on paradigm shifts.** We rebuilt the LLM statement score under a five-layer hallucination-audit protocol (retrieval consistency, fact-check, anchor-independence, multi-node voting across four vendor families, self-consistency; Appendix A) and find r = +0.351 with dictionary tone — overlapping, not redundant. The substance is in the disagreements: on eleven statements — Operation Twist, QE3, the 2015 calendar-guidance recalibration, the 2017 run-off, four consecutive 2020 pandemic-QE statements, the June-2022 acceleration — the dictionary output is nearly *constant* (std 1.10) while the audited LLM committee separates directions sharply (std ≈ 71; Figure 4). The dictionary does not mis-read these statements; it fails to differentiate them. Sign agreement across four vendor families on those eleven statements is 100%. We position the LLM measure as a diagnostic of dictionary failure, not a better scorer — it adds no price or flow predictiveness — but the diagnostic lands where it matters: the tone measure that dominates the statement-communication literature loses resolution exactly on the episodes the paradigm-shift narrative is about.

**Contribution.** Three claims. (i) *Identification*: the inversion rule, stated as a reproducible one-line classification over a public shock file, gives statement-level MP/CBI decomposition without proprietary infrastructure, and its predictions (prices, not flows) survive tests designed to kill them. (ii) *Mechanism*: the flow null is evidence, not absence — with a theoretical reason rooted in the timing structure (prices are instantaneous; flows are monthly; what survives to the monthly horizon is risk content, not words) and a reversal pattern in the window decomposition (safe-asset inflow in the same month, −0.99 unwinding in the difference window, p = 0.014) that pins the reallocation dynamics. (iii) *Measurement*: the resolution-loss signature, its five-layer audit, and cross-vendor confirmation discipline the tone literature precisely where its objects are moving.
