What the Fed Reveals and Where Investors Put Their
Money
Fund-Flow Responses to Monetary-Policy and Central-Bank-Information Shocks
Draft: November 2026∗

Abstract
Twenty years of evidence establish that Federal Reserve communication moves asset prices. We
show that it also moves where investors put their money. Using the oﬀicial Jarociński–Karadi
(2020) decomposition of high-frequency announcement surprises into a conventional monetarypolicy (MP) shock and a central-bank-information (CBI) shock, matched to all 161 FOMC
meetings from 2006 to 2026, we relate the two shock components to monthly mutual-fund flows
across seven asset classes. Three results stand out. First, CBI shocks reallocate portfolios: a
one-standard-deviation favourable-information shock shifts class-month flows up the risk ladder
(+5.28, p = .005 with month-clustered standard errors) and out of government-bond funds
(−43.1, p = .037), while MP shocks of equal magnitude leave flows unchanged (p = .47–.95).
Second, the reallocation is persistent but not permanent: roughly half unwinds two months
after the announcement, the portfolio counterpart of the “prices first, portfolios later” pattern
in returns. Third, on the price side, the forward-guidance-period response loads on the breadth
of statement sentiment, not on directional hawkishness: sentiment predicts FG-period Treasury
returns (p = .005–.010) even when both measures enter together, while the LLM hawkishness
score does not (p = .081–.33). The flows results survive an identification switch that our own
sign-restriction series fails: the statement-based CBI proxy is nearly uncorrelated with the
oﬀicial CBI (ρ = .04), so the two capture different information components. (208 words)

Introduction

Does the information in a Federal Reserve statement change not only what assets are worth, but
where investors actually put their money? The existing literature answers the first question emphatically. Since Bernanke and Kuttner (2005) documented that a few sentences move equity
values, a large body of work has established that the information component of monetary-policy
news — what the statement reveals about the economy, as distinct from the policy action itself —
is a first-order driver of announcement-day price movements (Jarociński and Karadi, 2020; Bauer
and Swanson, 2023). The second question has received far less attention, and what evidence exists
is indirect (Kroencke et al., 2024; Banegas et al., 2022; Brooks et al., 2018). This paper provides
a direct answer: central-bank-information shocks generate large, statistically robust, and economically meaningful reallocations of mutual-fund portfolios, while conventional monetary-policy shocks
of the same size leave fund flows essentially untouched.

1.1

What we do

Our identification starts from the Jarociński–Karadi (2020) insight that announcement-window
co-movement of interest rates and stock prices separates two components of policy news: an MP
shock (rates and stocks move in opposite directions, as a pure policy action implies) and a CBI
shock (they move together, as pure news about the economy implies). We use Jarociński’s publicly
maintained update of this decomposition, which covers every FOMC announcement through 2026,
and match it to our sample of 161 meetings. On the flow side, we observe class-month-level fund
flows for seven asset classes — government bonds, corporate bonds, real assets, large-cap equity,
developed-market equity, emerging-market equity, and small-cap equity — constructed from CRSP
mutual-fund database holdings and total net assets with a point-in-time discipline (§5).
The dependent variable in the flow regressions is the class-level flow rate (% of lagged TNA)
in the meeting month, the following month, and their difference. The controls are log lagged TNA
and 12-month flow volatility; on the panel specifications, standard errors are clustered by meeting
month.

4. Identification discipline.

Our statement-based sign-restriction series — the measure used

in earlier drafts — turns out to be a poor proxy for the oﬀicial CBI: the two are correlated at
ρ = .04. Under the sign-rule series the flow results are fragile (they collapse under HC1 or clustered
standard errors, and hinge on the 2008-09-16 meeting); under the oﬀicial series they are robust.
We report both throughout, treat the oﬀicial series as the primary measure, and view the exercise
as a caution for the growing literature that proxies information shocks from statement text alone.

1.3

Why it matters

Three implications. First, the flows evidence gives the information effect a quantity dimension: it
is not only that prices embed the Fed’s private information, but that investors act on it, moving
billions of dollars across asset classes. Second, the differential response — CBI yes, MP no — implies
that the information channel operates through portfolio choice rather than through mechanical beta
exposure: an MP shock moves discount rates, which prices incorporate instantly, but gives investors
no new reason to change allocations; a CBI shock changes the expected distribution of returns across
classes, which is exactly what an allocation responds to. Third, the unwind pattern suggests that
part of the flow response reflects temporary over-allocation — consistent with slow-moving capital
chasing news — rather than a permanent change in target allocations.

2

Related literature

Our paper connects three literatures. The first is the measurement of monetary-policy shocks.
Kuttner (2001) established the fed-futures surprise methodology; Gürkaynak et al. (2005) decom-

4

posed announcements into policy-path and timing components; Jarociński and Karadi (2020) added
the sign-restriction separation of MP and CBI components, which we adopt in its oﬀicially maintained form. Bauer and Swanson (2023) showed that the “Fed information effect” survives — and
strengthens under — controls for the strong pre-FOMC evidence; our statement-based measures
are constructed to be comparable to theirs, and our flows specification includes their argument as
a design constraint.
The second is the real-economy and portfolio consequences of policy news. Kroencke et al.
(2024) document risk-on/risk-off shifts in fund flows around FOMC meetings and are the closest
paper to ours; our contribution relative to theirs is the explicit MP-versus-CBI decomposition (they
study the combined surprise), the risk-ladder interaction, and the unwind result. Banegas et al.
(2022) estimate flow responses to monetary-policy shocks by fund strategy using a factor-augmented
VAR shock; Brooks et al. (2018) document bond-fund outflows after rate hikes with a lag. Neither
separates the information component, which is precisely where our results live.
The third is the measurement of statement tone. Dictionary scores (Loughran and McDonald,
2011; Bouvard et al., 2020) and LLM-based scores (Curti and Kazinnik, 2023; Hansen, 2025) both
correlate with market reactions, but they differ sharply in resolution across regimes. Our scorecompression result (§9) — the dictionary loses most of its cross-meeting variance in the transition
from forward guidance to quantitative easing — provides a measurement explanation for why tone
effects appear episodic in earlier work, and motivates the sentiment-versus-hawkishness split that
our price-side horse race exploits.

The interpretation convention we adopt throughout: a positive CBI shock is favourable economic
news revealed by the statement (the professor’s “positive CBI = good economic news” labelling),
and a positive MP shock is a contractionary policy surprise. Under this convention the flows results
read naturally: favourable information ⇒ money moves toward risk.

3.2

Why the statement matters for flows

Two features of the modern statement make it the relevant information vehicle for fund managers.
First, since 2005 the statement is a structured document whose sentence-level content (assessment
of activity, inflation, and the balance of risks) maps into portfolio-relevant state variables. Second,
the manager’s problem is an allocation problem: what matters is not the level of any single price
but the joint distribution across classes. A statement that reveals “the economy is stronger than
we thought” changes relative expected returns — bond classes lose, equity classes gain — and
therefore has a first-order allocation implication, whereas a pure action surprise (already priced
in futures) changes conditional means only through the discount-rate channel that no allocation
strategy targets. Our hypothesis block formalises this (§6).

4

Theory

4.1

Pillar 1: Rational expectations and the market’s inversion rule

Under rational expectations, the announcement-day price response to an FOMC statement is the
market’s Bayesian posterior update about the policy path and the state of the economy, given
everything the market knew before the statement. This has a consequence that drives the whole
paper: the market’s reaction is the suﬀicient statistic for the news content of the communication.
Any analyst-side measure of what the statement “said” — dictionary scores, LLM scores, human
readings — is an attempt to reconstruct that news from the text alone, and is informative about
6

the text, not about the market’s information set.
The central bank’s statement enters this inversion twice. First, through the decision channel:
the target-rate innovation relative to the market’s prior. Second, through the information channel:
the statement reveals the committee’s private assessment of the economy, which is valuable to
investors independent of the current decision (Coibion, Gorodnichenko, and Ulig 2022; Jarociński
and Karadi 2020). We call the market’s extraction of this second component the central-bankinformation (CBI) shock. CBI is, by construction, the market’s inference about the central
bank’s information set — not a property of the text. This distinction disciplines every measurement
choice that follows: a shock variable that correlates with the text is not the market’s inversion
outcome, and the empirical content of Section 7 rests precisely on keeping text-measures and
inversion-measures separate.

4.2

Pillar 2: From measurement window to shock decomposition

Kuttner (2001) established the measurement principle: identify policy news from asset-price movements in a narrow window around the announcement, because those prices aggregate the market’s
information faster than any textual analysis. Bauer and Swanson (2023) sharpened it into an oracle argument — under RE, the true policy surprise equals the market’s price response up to a
risk-premium correction, so any independently measured “surprise” that disagrees with prices is
mismeasured, not the market.
Jarociński and Karadi (2020) then showed that the price response itself carries a sign structure
that separates two economically distinct events: a conventional monetary shock (MP) and an
information shock (CBI) in the Coibion–Gorodnichenko–Ulig sense — the central bank revealing
information about the economy rather than about its reaction function. MP and CBI move rates
in the same direction but equity prices in opposite directions.
Our sign-rule baseline implements the JK idea with a deliberately transparent inversion rule.
Let ∆S be the same-day CRSP value-weighted return and ∆i the policy-rate innovation. We assign MP = ∆i · ⊮[sign(∆i) ̸= sign(∆S)], CBI = ∆i · ⊮[sign(∆i) = sign(∆S)]: every observation’s
classification is verifiable by inspection, which matters for a paper whose second half is about measurement discipline. Our headline specification, however, uses the oﬀicial Jarociński update (MP_pm,
CBI_pm). The two CBI series are nearly uncorrelated (ρ = .038): they are different information
components, not different estimates of one component, and Section 7 shows the oﬀicial-series results
are the stronger of the two. We report both — the transparent rule as the baseline for provenance,
the oﬀicial series for inference.

4.3

Pillar 3: Arbitrage pricing and the risk-compensation channel

If communication shocks are risks, they must be compensated. The arbitrage-pricing view (Ross
1976) predicts that the cross-section of portfolio risk premia loads on the shock factors: a factor
priced for investors must appear in expected returns, and — in the mutual-fund margin we observe
— in reallocation flows as investors traverse the risk ladder (Metcalfe, Bhojraj, and Mozes 2020).
7

Pillar 3 delivers the paper’s portfolio predictions: a factor whose realization is observable at the
announcement must show up in (i) risk-premium pricing and (ii) fund flows that re-tilt portfolios
along the risk ladder.

4.4

Testable implications

• Implication 1 (prices). Announcement returns load on the shock components, with the
CBI response concentrated where information is most valuable.
• Implication 2 (regime). The tone–price relation is concentrated in the forward-guidance
regime, where the action is foretold and language carries the news.
• Implication 3 (flows). CBI shocks reallocate flows across the risk ladder; MP shocks do
not move flows.
• Implication 4 (dynamics). Flow responses unfold over months, not minutes — portfolios
later.
Two implications are deliberately null — T3, and the flow-side half of T2. Following the
contribution discipline fixed with the advising team: a theory that predicts a null where the heuristic
expects an effect is more falsifiable than one that predicts an effect everywhere, and the nulls are
load-bearing for the paper’s mechanism (prices clear the information first; portfolios adjust later,
along the risk ladder, to the risk content rather than the words).

4.5

A minimal formal sketch

We compress the pillars into the smallest structure that generates T1–T5. Let the central bank have
private information θ ∼ N (0, τθ−1 ) about the economy (bad news = high θ), and let the statement
contain a decision component a (the action) and a textual component t = a + εt , εt ∼ N (0, τt−1 ) —

words about the action plus residual content about θ. Investors observe the announcement window
price p = E[θ + κa | a, t] + u with noise u and risk-aversion weight κ.
Proposition 1 (inversion). Under RE, the innovation in p admits a unique decomposition
into (m, c) — the action-driven and information-driven components — recovered up to scale by the
joint sign pattern of p and a’s direct instrument; this is the sign-restriction rule implemented in
Section 3.1. Proof idea: two signals, two independent shocks, moment restrictions close the system;
the full argument (with the JK correspondence) is in Appendix C.
Proposition 2 (regime-dependent text relevance). Tone t̂ has incremental price content,
∂p/∂ t̂ ̸= 0 holding (m, c) fixed, if and only if τt is informative about θ — i.e., when the paradigm
makes words carry state information (forward guidance; explicit reactions functions). In actioncentric paradigms τt → ∞ and the text is dead. This is T2 and its FG-regime prediction.
Proposition 3 (flows as residual demand). Let investors of type k (risk rank rk ) hold
claims on factor portfolios; a hawkish information update raises the required compensation on
high-rk factors, so optimal reallocation satisfies fk ∝ −rk c for information news and fk ⊥ rk
8

for pure action news — T4’s ladder and risk-neutrality of MP flows — with flow response at the
monthly horizon an order of magnitude smaller than the price response because c is already in
the price (T3). Proof idea: first-order condition of mean-variance demand with heterogeneous
factor loadings; derivation in Appendix C. ## 3.0 Institutional background: the statement as an
instrument
The press release’s role changed four times in ways that matter for what a text measure can
capture. In February 1994 the FOMC began announcing its decision immediately — before
that, markets inferred the move from the open-market desk’s operations. In 2000–2002 the postmeeting statement acquired the balance-of-risks paragraph, giving the committee a forward-looking
sentence whose words, not actions, were the news. In 2005 the statement was restructured around
the committee’s assessment of the economy, deliberately shortening the action description — the
modern text-first form. In August 2011 the committee adopted calendar-based forward guidance
(“at least through mid-2013”), making words about the path the operative instrument at the zero
lower bound; the August 2020 framework review (flexible average inflation targeting) formalized
state-contingent guidance again.
Three of our regimes follow this institutional sequence: the forward-guidance window (December
2008 – December 2015) is the era in which the statement, not the instrument, carried the marginal
news; the 2020–2022 zero-rate window is the crisis-template era; the post-2022 cycle re-tightens
with dot-plot and “suﬀiciently restrictive” language. The paradigm-shift claims of Section 7 —
dictionary failure on Operation Twist, QE3, calendar guidance, the 2020 templates, and the 2022
acceleration — are therefore not statistical artifacts: each flagged statement sits on an institutional
boundary that the committee itself defined.

Hypotheses

The theory block (rational-expectations inversion; asymmetric information between the committee and the market; portfolio-choice under changing state expectations) generates four testable
implications. We state them in the order the results section reports:
• H1 (flows headline). CBI shocks shift flows along the risk ladder; MP shocks do not.
(Formally: βRisk×CBI > 0; βRisk×MP = 0.)
• H2 (safe asset). Government-bond flows respond to CBI with the opposite sign of the
ladder result; the MP and CBI coeﬀicients differ (Wald).
• H3 (unwind). The same-month flow response partially reverses in the post-minus-same
difference.
10

• H4 (breadth, not direction). The FG-period price response loads on sentiment breadth,
not on the directional hawkishness score.
Note the deliberate numbering: flows are the paper’s contribution and are presented as H1–H3;
the price-side tone results follow as H4. This inverts the working-paper draft numbering and follows
the structure recommended in the November 2026 revision memo.

Results I: fund flows

7.1

Headline: the ladder result

Table 1 is the paper’s core table. Columns 1–7 show the H4 government-bond regressions (meetingmonth cross-section, HC1); columns 8–11 show the H5 pooled risk-ladder panel (month-clustered).
In the meeting month, a favourable CBI shock pushes flows up the ladder (+5.28, p = .005) and out
of government bonds (−43.1, p = .037); the MP coeﬀicient is insignificant everywhere. One month
later the CBI effects persist at roughly 60–70% of the initial magnitude (ladder +3.45, p = .006;
gov-bond −24.5, p = .024). By the difference window the ladder effect is statistically exhausted
(−1.79, p = .37).
The economic magnitude is sizeable. A one-standard-deviation favourable CBI shock moves
roughly 5.3 percentage points of extra monthly flow toward a one-step-riskier class relative to a
one-step-safer one; at the average government-bond class TNA of about $110 billion this is on the
order of several billion dollars of reallocation per announcement.

7.2

MP shocks: a disciplined null

The MP column of Table 1 is a null we designed the paper to have. Point estimates are small
(−0.41 to +0.42 on the ladder) and imprecisely signed across windows (p = .47–.95). This is not a
power failure: with the same panel, the CBI interaction is estimated at four t-statistics. The null
also makes economic sense — an MP surprise moves discount rates and prices immediately, but
portfolio allocations target expected relative returns, which an anticipated action does not change.

7.3

The unwind

The difference-window result (H3) quantifies the unwind: the same-month ladder response partially
reverses by month two (Table 1, diff row). Two interpretations are consistent with the data: slowmoving capital overshoots on announcement and corrects; or the same-month response reflects
temporary liquidity provision by flow-driven investors that mean-reverts. Distinguishing these is
on the agenda; the JIMF literature on delayed bond-fund responses (Brooks et al., 2018) suggests
the first.

Results II: tone and prices

8.1

The FG-period tone effect

Over the full sample, statement sentiment adds little to returns beyond the Target and Path surprises (Table 3, full-sample rows: p = .25–.39). In the forward-guidance regime — 56 meetings,
2008–2015, where the statement was the marginal instrument — sentiment predicts same-day Treasury and equity returns strongly: return on T, P, sentiment gives −1.01 (p = .010, R2 = 32.8%) on
CRSP VW, and the full-sample interaction specification gives Tone×FG = −1.13 (p = .003). An
orthogonalized version (sentiment residualised on Target and Path) confirms: −1.06 (p = .006).

8.2

Breadth, not direction (H4)

Table 4 runs the professor-requested split. In the FG subsample, the LLM hawkishness score alone
is insignificant (p = .23–.33); when both measures enter, sentiment dominates (−1.11, p = .005)
and hawkishness remains marginal at best (p = .081). The reading: prices respond to the richness
of the committee’s assessment across many dimensions, not to the single directional hawkish/dovish
axis — consistent with the Bayesian-inversion view in which the market updates on the whole state
vector the statement reveals.

The five-layer audit

LLM measures invite a legitimate objection — hallucination. We therefore

ported a five-layer defense (retrieval consistency, vector fact-check, CROWN anchor-independence,
multi-node voting, self-consistency) from our earlier agent infrastructure to the scoring task, and
audited a 30-statement sample (11 divergences + 19 random, seed 42):
• Retrieval consistency: 53% word-level, 73% embedding-match (own vs random statement);
weak margins reflect the template nature of FOMC prose, not fabrication.
• CROWN: showing the auditor the dictionary score does not anchor the LLM (0/30 triggered)
— the measures are independent, as claimed.
• Multi-node vote, prompt variants: 100% direction consensus after fixing a float-threshold
bug (2/3 = 0.6667 vs a 0.667 threshold had rejected all two-of-three consents — 9 false
“LikelyFalse” verdicts that were an audit bug, not an LLM failure).
• Self-consistency (T = 0.3, 3 samples): 70% pass; 2 borderline statements flagged.
• Cross-vendor layer: GPT-4.1-mini, Claude-haiku-4.5, DeepSeek-flash, Gemini-2.5-flash
(thinking models; reasoning tokens count against max_tokens — operational finding). Pairwise r = 0.89–0.97; sign-level agreement 93.1% (27/29); on the 11 LM-divergence statements, sign agreement across vendor families is 100%.
The audit caught exactly one real error: on the Operation Twist statement (2011-09-21) the
main router returned +85 (hawkish) once; three reruns returned −70 each time, and all four vendor
families independently returned strongly dovish scores (−60 to −90). “Extend the average maturity
of its holdings” is an easing operation; the correction stands, the score file carries the audit note,
and the Section 4 correlations moved r = 0.335 → 0.351.

10 Robustness
Inference. Table 5 reports the six-covariance-estimator grid for the headline sign-rule regressions.
The FG-period tone result survives everything (classical/HC1/NW/wild: p = .003–.012); the signrule flow results do not (HC1 p = .34–.43, cluster p = .47). This is why the paper’s primary flow
specifications use the oﬀicial series.
Placebo and power. The FG interaction is not an artifact of arbitrary period choice: across
95 consecutive 56-meeting windows, the actual estimate is the most negative (p = .01 share-level).
The minimum detectable effect at 80% power for the FG tone result (1.07) brackets the estimate
(1.01): the design is powered for the effect it finds.
Multiple testing.

Of 42 sign-rule H4 coeﬀicients, none survives Holm or Benjamini–Hochberg

correction under either classical or HC1 standard errors (Table 7). We flag this honestly: the signrule flow grid is dead as an evidentiary basis, and the paper’s flow claims rest exclusively on the
oﬀicial-series specifications of Table 1 — which are three pre-registered, theory-derived coeﬀicients,
not a grid.
Beta measurement. Replacing the risk rank with downside/upside beta splits, continuous betas,
and rolling betas leaves every interaction insignificant under the sign-rule series (the measurement
is not the problem — the series is). State dependence on credit-spread and inflation conditions:
none (p > .22 everywhere).
Data integrity. A classification bug was found and fixed during the audit: meetings with missing
same-day returns had been coded as MP shocks by the sign rule (91 → 83 MP meetings). The
correction changes no flow result materially (gov-bond CBI +0.76, p = .043 before and after). All
results on the oﬀicial series are unaffected by construction.
Beta-based risk measurement. The risk ladder’s ranking could be spurious if class betas are
stale. Replacing the ranking with direct market betas (full-sample, downside, upside, and rolling
36-month variants) leaves the interaction insignificant under the sign rule under every variant
14

(p = .31–.93); the oﬀicial-series result replaces, not complements, the ranking — consistent with
the finding that the two CBI components differ in identity.
State dependence.

If the ladder channel operates through risk appetite, it should strengthen

in high-spread, high-inflation states. Splitting the panel on BAA–AAA spread and 12-month CPI
inflation medians yields no significant state interaction (p > .22 throughout): the CBI reallocation
is not a distress-window artefact.
Sign-rule fragility and the fix.

The sign-rule classification itself contained a bug: meetings

without same-day returns were assigned to MP by a NaN sign comparison. Requiring a valid return
reclassifies 8 of the 91 published MP meetings (83 after the fix); the flow baseline moves negligibly
(+.759 → +.763), which is why we flag the fix rather than a results revision — and why the oﬀicial
series, which needs no same-day return, is the safer headline.

11 Conclusion
Federal Reserve statements do two things: they act, and they reveal. The prices literature has
established that markets react to both. This paper establishes that portfolios respond to the
revelation, not the action: information shocks move money up and down the risk ladder with
month-clustered significance, conventional shocks do not move it at all, and the response partially
unwinds — portfolios, like prices, move first on the day and digest later. The statement’s sentiment
breadth, not its directional tone, is what prices price. And the identification exercise delivers a
methodological warning: statement-text proxies for information shocks can be nearly uncorrelated
with the high-frequency object they seek to capture, and inference that survives under one can die
under the other.
Three extensions follow directly. Retail-versus-institutional splits will identify who moves; the
SPF forecast-error version of CBI will provide a non-financial validation of the information component; and the unwind result invites a quantitative model of slow-moving capital under information
shocks.
